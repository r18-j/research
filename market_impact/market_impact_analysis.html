Market Impact Analysis

Raj Kundi

Introduction

Issue: for the optimal order execution and market making models, transaction costs are a significant factor in the performance.

Costs not only impact realised returns of active investment strategies, it also has an effect on the unwinding rate of optimal execution models since this unwinding faster costs more.

Costs generally break down into: - direct costs are commissions and fees, and - indirect costs are costs that are not explicitly stated.

For large trades, the most important component is the impact of the trader’s own actions on the market.

Data

Clean data

Remove trades during periods of extreme volatility, and remove trades too small to be of any significance and only add noise.

Variables

Goal: to describe market impact in terms of a small number of input variables.

Observable/measurements

The following price points are of interest:

  • market price \(S_0\) before the order begins executing;
  • market price \(S_\text{post}\) after the order is complete, and
  • average realized price \(\tilde{S}\) on the order.

The mid-price is the market (reference) price.

The post trade price is used to capture the permanent effects of the trade. It should be taken long enough after the last execution where any effects of temporary liquidity effects have dissipated. (The paper recommends about one half-hour after the last execution is adequate to achieve this. Investigate a time using The long memory of the efficient market.) So, we define \[ t_\text{post} = t_n + \text{30 mins}. \] We can now define the permanent impact and realised impact by \[ \begin{array}{rrcl} \text{Permanent impact:} & I & = & \frac{S_\text{post} - S_\text{pre}}{S_\text{pre}}, \\ \text{Realized impact:} & J & = & \frac{S_\text{realized} - S_\text{pre}}{S_\text{pre}}. \end{array} \] The “effective” impact \(J\) determines the actual cash received or spend on the trade.

To define temporary impact we will look at \(J\) minus a suitable fraction of \(I\), and the temporary impact is not observable, but can be theoretically measured in this model.

The impacts \(I\) and \(J\) may be positive or negative, but generally depends on the total order \(X\). For buy orders, then \(X \gt 0\), and positive cost means that the price \(S(t)\) moves upwards. So, generally the sign of \(X\) should be the same as \(I\) and \(J\).

Volume time

The market activity is known to vary throughout the day, but does so consistently between different periods of the trading day. This affects both the volume profile and variance of prices. To minimise its impact, we perform analysis under a volume clock, using volume time \(\tau\), which is the fraction of an average day’s volume that has executed up to clock time \(t\). (NB: the volume time \(\tau\) is normalised, started from 0 and finishing at 1 for the day.)

We map each of the clock times \(t_0, \ldots, t_n\) in the trades data to a corresponding volume tick \(\tau_0, \ldots, \tau_n\). For liquid venues, we can use nonparametric estimators that directly measures differences in \(\tau\): the shares traded during the period corresponding to the execution of each order.

Fit a cubic on the intraday volume data.

Explanatory variables

To explain the impacts \(I\) and \(J\), consider the quantities:

  • total executed quantities \(X\) (which for now is just each trades size);
  • volume duration of active trading, \(T = \tau_n - \tau_0\) (which for now is just 0, since all trades are isntant 1 strike trades), and
  • volume duration of impact, \(T_\text{post} = \tau_\text{post} - \tau_0\).

As noted, the total executed quantity is positive for a buy order and negative for a sell order.

Auxiliary vairables

Despite trying to explain the dependence of the impact costs \(I\) and \(J\) on the order size and (volume) trade time, other market variables will influence the solution:

  • average daily volume in shares \(V\), and
  • daily volatility \(\sigma\).

The average daily volume is calculated by a ten-day moving average. The volatility uses an intraday estimator that makes use of every transaction in the day. This needs to be tracked between different stocks.

So \(V\) is the ten day average intraday volume profile, and the volatility on fifteenth minute intervals.

These serve to normalise the active/explanatory variables. For example the order size as a fraction of average volume traded during the time of execution. We can estimate \(VT\) directly by taking the average volume that executed between clock times \(t_0\) and \(t_n\) over the previous ten days. This is the only way this appears in the model, so we don’t need to estimate \(T\) directly.

We use volatility to scale the impacts: a certain level of participation in the daily volume should cause certain level of participation in the normal motion of the asset.

Trajectory cost model

Simplifactions:

  • the rate of trading is constant (in volume time), and
  • neglect relations between stocks.

We decompose the price impact into two components: - a permanent component that reflects the information transmitted to the market by the buy/sell imbalance. - A temporary component reflecting the price concession needed to attract counterparties to trade with.

The realised price impact is a combination of these two effects. \[ \text{realized} = \text{permanent} + \text{temporary} \]

With suitable coefficients and scaling depending on \(T\). So temporary impact can be obtained as the different.

So, we start with an initial desired order of \(X\) shares. We assume that this order is completed by uniform rate of trading over a volume time interval \(T\). That is, the trade rate in volume units is \(v = X/T\), and is held constant until the program is completed. Constant rate in these units is equivalent to VWAP execution during the time of execution.

Permanent and temporary impact

Permanent impact

The model postulates that the asset price \(S(\tau)\) follows an arithmetic Brownian motion with a drift term that depnds on our trade rate \(v\).

\[ dS = S_0 g(v) d \tau + S_0 \sigma d B \] where \(B(\tau)\) is a standard Brownian motion, and \(g(v)\) is the permanent impact function. As noted, \(\tau\) is volume time, representing the fraction average of an average day’s volume that has executed so far. Integrating this expression in time, taking $ v = X/T$ for \(0 \le \tau \le T\), then \[ I = T g \left( \frac{X}{T} \right) + \sigma \sqrt{T_\text{post}} \xi \] where \(\xi\) is a standard Guassian variate.

Temporary impact

The price actually received from out trades is \[ \tilde{S}(\tau) = S(\tau) + S_0 h\left( \frac{X}{T} \right) \] where \(h(v)\) is the temporary impact function.

At constant liquidation rate, calculating the time average of the execution price gives the temporary impact expression \[ J - \frac{I}{2} = h\left(\frac{H}{T}\right) + \sigma \left( \sqrt{\frac{T}{12}\left(4 - 3\frac{T}{T_\text{post}}\right)}\,\chi - \frac{T_\text{post} - T}{2\sqrt{T_\text{post}}}\,\xi \right) \]

where \(\chi\) is also a standard Gaussian variate, independent to \(\xi\).

These equations give us explicit expressions for the permanent and temporary cost componenets \(I\) and \(J\) in terms of the values of the functions \(g\) and \(h\) at known trade rates, together with estimates of the magnitude of the error coming from volatility.

Choice of functional form

We postulate that these functions are power laws, that is,

\[ \begin{array}{rcl} g(v) & = & \pm \gamma |v|^\alpha, \\ h(v) & = & \pm \eta |v|^\beta, \end{array} \] where numerical values of the coefficients and exponents are to be determined by linear and nonlinear regression on the data. The sign is to be chosen to have the same sign as \(v\).

Cross sectional description

In the preceding discussion, we assumed a single asset, for which we assumed that changes in impact functions from one day to the next were adequately handled by scaling the impact functions by daily volume and volatility. But single asset fits are only marginally significant for practical use.

Parameter scaling

We have assumed an ideal asset, all of whose properties are constant in time. For any real asset, the parameters that determine market impact will vary with time. For example, if the volume for a day is unusually low, we would have higher costs. Or if the volatility was unusually hight, again, that would increase costs.

We therefore assume that the natural variable for characterising the size of an overall order or of a rate of trading is not shares per se but the number of shares relative to a best estimate of the background flow for that stock in the time period when trading will occur. That is, the impact cost functions should be expressed in terms of the dimensionless quantity \(X/V T\) rather than \(X\) itself, where \(V\) is the average number of shares per day.

Furthermore, we suppose the motion of the price should not be given as a raw percentage figure, but should be expressed as a fraction of the normal daily motion of the price, as expressed by the volatility \(\sigma\).

Therefore, \[ \begin{array}{rcl} I & = & \sigma T g\left( \frac{X}{VT} \right) \\ J - \frac{I}{2} & = & \sigma h \left( \frac{X}{VT} \right) \end{array} \]

Temporary

Through experimenting, the temporary cost function does not require any stock-specific modification.

Determination of exponent

After assuming the functional forms, by performing a nonlinear regression, we determing the values of \(\alpha, \beta\) and \(\gamma\) in: \[ \begin{array}{rcl} \frac 1 \sigma & = & \gamma T \text{sgn} (X) \left| \frac{X}{VT} \right|^\alpha \left( \frac{\Theta}{V} \right) ^ \gamma \\ \frac{1}{\sigma} \left( J - \frac 1 2 \right) & = & \eta \text{sgn}(X) \left| \frac{X}{VT} \right|^\beta \end{array} \]

The error/confidence-intervals are best expressed by exploring one standard deviation away. Then the true value can be expected to be within this range with 67% probability and within a range twice as large with 95% probability.
